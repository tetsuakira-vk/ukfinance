Boundary disputes with neighbours can quickly turn into expensive headaches — and the question of who pays for a new fence is one of the most common flashpoints. If you're thinking about installing a fence but aren't sure who should foot the bill, here's what you need to know.

**Check your title deeds first**

When you buy a house, your title deeds or title register (held by the Land Registry in England and Wales) should show who is responsible for maintaining existing boundaries. Look for a 'T' marked on the boundary on your title plan — this indicates which homeowner is legally responsible for that fence or wall.

However, many older properties have unclear boundaries, especially terraced houses where the title deeds don't specify exactly where the legal line falls. If this is your situation, you're not alone, and it doesn't automatically mean the cost should be split.

**The key rule: the person who installs it pays**

Here's the important bit: if you want a new fence that your neighbours haven't asked for, you'll likely be responsible for the entire cost of buying and installing it — even if it's on the boundary line. Your neighbours don't have to contribute unless they've agreed to in writing.

If the fence does blow down in the wind later, you'll probably be liable for replacing it, since you put it up.

**Getting neighbours to share costs**

If your neighbours do agree to split the cost, get this agreement in writing. Clearly state who pays what, and ideally have them sign a document. This protects everyone if disputes arise later about maintenance or replacement costs.

If neighbours refuse to contribute but the fence benefits them (for example, it blocks an unsightly view), you might still push back — but ultimately, if they didn't ask for it, you're on shakier legal ground.

**Next steps**

Before you start, contact the Land Registry to confirm your boundaries. If they're still unclear and all neighbours agree, you can hire a solicitor to formally define them — though this costs money. For a straightforward fence installation, get quotes from local fencing contractors and have that neighbour agreement in writing before work begins.

Consider speaking to a property solicitor if boundaries are genuinely disputed — it's cheaper than a court battle later.
