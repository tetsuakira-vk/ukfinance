If you're claiming Child Benefit and earning over £60,000 a year, you'll need to repay some of it to HMRC. But many parents are confused about how much they actually owe — and the official calculator isn't always matching their own workings. Here's what you need to know.

The High Income Child Benefit Charge kicks in when your individual income exceeds £60,000 annually. You have to repay 1% of your Child Benefit for every £200 you earn above that threshold. If either you or your partner hits £80,000, you'll need to repay the entire amount.

Currently, Child Benefit is worth £27.05 per week for your first child (£1,406.60 yearly) and £17.90 per week for each additional child (£930.80 yearly). So if you have two children and earn £80,000 or more, you're looking at paying back around £2,337.40 annually.

**Why the calculator might show a different figure**

HMRC has clarified that discrepancies between the official calculator and manual calculations often come down to one simple issue: exact dates. The calculator needs to know precisely when your Child Benefit claim started — particularly if you didn't receive it for a full year. If you began claiming part-way through the tax year (for instance, when your baby was born), you need to enter the correct start date.

The tax authority also doesn't tax fractions of a pound. So if your calculator shows a reduction of 68p compared to your manual sum, that's not a rounding error — it's HMRC simply not charging you for amounts under £1.

**What you should do**

You can use HMRC's free Child Benefit calculator on the government website. Make sure you enter the precise amount of Child Benefit you've received and use the exact dates your claim began and ended (if applicable). HMRC has confirmed: "The calculator will give you the right figure, just make sure you're using the same start date as we used to calculate your claim."

If the calculator still doesn't match your expectations, double-check those dates first. If you're still unsure, contact HMRC directly — they can explain which figure is correct for your circumstances.
